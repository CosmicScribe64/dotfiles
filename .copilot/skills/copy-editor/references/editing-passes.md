# Focused editing passes

These are the broad review guides retained from v2. The 30 individually runnable
checks use [pass-catalog.md](pass-catalog.md) and the exact prompt exported by the
workbench. A broad review does not complete every named check in its category.

Use only the requested pass. The prohibitions on replacement wording and praise
apply to every pass. A checklist item is a question to investigate, not a fault to
find. Tie advice to the text, audience, and purpose.

## Structure and argument

Check whether readers learn the central claim or narrative direction when they
need it. Identify prerequisites introduced too late, unsupported jumps,
competing paragraph functions, and conclusions stronger than their premises.
Separate what a source supports from the writer's inference.

For each paragraph, consider its function: claim, example, evidence, qualification,
context, counterargument, or consequence. Repeated functions can be deliberate;
flag them only when they obstruct the piece or duplicate work.

For a move, name an existing paragraph and destination by ID, explain which
reader question it would answer sooner, and identify any damaged chronology,
setup/payoff, or transition. Suggest at most two or three moves with a clear
reader benefit unless the text requires broader restructuring. Do not invent a
move to meet that limit or write the new transition.

For a gap, state the information or reasoning the author needs to supply. Do not
supply the missing argument or factual answer on their behalf.

## Clarity: actors, actions, and references

Look for sentences where readers cannot tell who acts, what happens, what a
pronoun refers to, or how clauses relate. Identify concrete competing readings.

Inspect passive constructions only for a reader problem: missing responsibility,
an unnecessarily delayed actor, or disrupted topic continuity. Passive voice can
be suitable when the actor is unknown, unimportant, or intentionally de-emphasized.
Do not flag every form of "to be" as passive.

Inspect nominalizations when actions become abstract nouns and the reader must
reconstruct agency or sequence. Do not label every noun ending in a particular
suffix a defect; technical concepts sometimes need stable noun names. Diagnose
buried action without supplying its verb form as replacement wording.

Look for long interruptions between subject and verb, overloaded clauses, nested
qualifications, and ambiguous modifiers. Sentence length alone is not evidence.
Check whether familiar information prepares new information and whether the end
of a sentence puts weight on the intended point. Present these as contextual
heuristics, not mandatory sentence templates.

## Economy and emphasis

Check for repeated claims without added work, redundant setup, throat-clearing,
metacommentary, excessive qualification, and details that distract from the
piece's purpose. For redundancy, cite the passage that already does that work.

Inspect intensifiers and stance markers such as "very," "really," "actually,"
and "unfortunately" when they occur in the author's draft. Determine whether each
adds degree, contrast, attitude, rhythm, or voice. Do not impose a ban or substitute
a supposedly stronger word. Identify candidates for a deletion test without
printing the resulting sentence.

For a cut, explain both the likely benefit and any lost humor, qualification,
precision, characterization, or pacing. Do not demand a universal percentage
reduction. Quantify savings only from an actual count of specific text, and label
estimates as estimates.

## Diction, repetition, and rhythm

Look for repeated phrases or sentence openings, abstract clusters, inconsistent
terms for the same thing, unexplained register changes, strained metaphors, and
references that require knowledge the audience may not share.

Distinguish accidental repetition from a refrain, a necessary technical term, or
deliberate emphasis. Do not invent synonyms merely to vary vocabulary. Flag
rhythm only when it causes a specific reading problem; uniform sentence variety
is not a goal in itself.

Do not remove slang, jokes, profanity, dialect, or unusual phrasing to make the
piece generically professional. If a reference may exclude part of the stated
audience, identify the assumed knowledge and the tradeoff rather than rewriting
it into a safer joke.

## Mechanics and consistency

Check agreement, grammatical attachment, punctuation, spelling, duplicated words,
capitalization, tense, number formatting, and citation consistency. Respect the
writer's dialect, medium, and chosen style guide; a convention is not universal.

For a mechanical issue, quote the problematic original span, name the rule or
inconsistency, and ask the author to correct it. Do not provide a corrected form
or a patch. A fragment in a personal essay or text message may be intentional.
When uncertain, flag a verification question rather than assert an error.

## Diagnostic examples

These examples are diagnostic comments, not draft text. In a review, use
locations and evidence from the author's text.

Allowed:

> P4 introduces a consequence before P7 explains its cause. Test moving P7 before
> P4; check whether this reveals information the later anecdote needs to withhold.

Allowed:

> P2 names two organizations before using the pronoun in the quoted sentence.
> Decide which organization performs the action and make that referent explicit.

Allowed:

> P5 repeats the conclusion already established in P3 without adding evidence.
> Test removing it; retain it if the repetition is needed for the spoken cadence.

Not allowed: following one of these diagnoses with a rewritten sentence,
suggested transition, alternative phrase, corrected copy, or praise.
